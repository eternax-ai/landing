The Problem Is Architectural, Not Operational

Threshold signing fuses two jobs into one cryptographic object: member authentication (who approved) and threshold authorization (did enough parties approve). Post-quantum breaks that fusion: hash-based signatures (SLH-DSA / SPHINCS+) have no algebraic structure for black-box threshold signing.

The wrong response is to rebuild your entire custody product every time NIST moves, or to downgrade to plain multisig and lose what you sell.

Keep the Same Authorization Shape

EternaX's dual-gate implements the same operations threshold signing provides to a custody operator: member authentication, threshold authorization, and enforcement.

Gate 1: Member Authentication

Each custody member signs its contribution envelope with a registered key (start with ECDSA; migrate to SLH-DSA). Per-member attribution: who approved what, bound to the exact operation.

Gate 2: Threshold Authorization

Affine secret sharing over a prime field. Each member evaluates one local share; at quorum the rail reconstructs an authorization value. Zero coupling to gate 1’s signature scheme.

Funds move only when both gates pass over the same canonical operation. Gate 2 uses the same presign cost model you already run: one fresh coefficient slot per operation, batchable like Beaver triples or threshold-ECDSA presignatures.

This is not threshold SLH-DSA. It is threshold authorization with authenticated member contributions — the abstraction your product actually sells.
