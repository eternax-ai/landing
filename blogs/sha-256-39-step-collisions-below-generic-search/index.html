Pushing Collision Attacks on 39-Step SHA-256 Below Generic Search

October 21, 2026

Author: Dariia Porechna, cryptographer, co-founder of EternaX

At a glance

Figure What it means

about 2127.6 vs 2128 The attack under the harshest accounting vs the birthday bound (still below it)

In October 2026 a collision attack on SHA-256 reduced to 39 of its 64 steps came in below generic search, to our knowledge for the first time. It started from my row-16 claw construction, first filed on 16 October, which turned the published 39-step differential trail of ePrint 2026/1120 into a differential attack at about the cost of generic search. Within three days three other researchers, working under the handles sraiw, Jbenisek (aka CyberAxe) and lamb356, extended and optimized it. On the board I file as dariolina. The version the board's reviewers accepted costs about 2118.35 SHA-256 compression evaluations against 2124.83 for the best accepted generic search under the same accounting. Two later filings, still in review, keep that program and only measure its tail more tightly, bringing the figure to 2118.15. Even under the harshest accounting, at about 2127.6, it stays below the 2128 birthday bound. The paper the construction starts from had concluded that its own method could not reach 39-step SHA-256: by its count, the attack would cost 2168.

The work was done on HashSmash, an open competition launched on 5 October 2026 by Eigen Labs and Shielded Labs to test how far AI agents can push attacks on SHA-256, SHA-3, BLAKE3 and Poseidon. Every package in this result was written with AI coding agents and checked by the board's AI judge; lamb356's filing was accepted by the board's human reviewers on 19 October.

NB: This is not a break of SHA-256. No colliding pair from the standard IV exists, 2118 compression evaluations is far beyond any computation that will ever be run, and the work has not yet been peer-reviewed. Full SHA-256 has 64 steps, so 25 steps of security margin remain.

When frontier AI agents were pointed at a heavily studied hash function in public, the attack frontier moved by one step. In the same year, AI-assisted research led to the withdrawal of a lattice-based signature candidate from NIST's standardization process. For anyone choosing post-quantum signatures, I think that contrast matters; the section "Post-AI & quantum cryptography" below compares what each family assumes.

The rest of this post explains how the attack works, which parts are mine and which are my co-filers', what the number rests on, and what comes next.

Prior work

For more than a decade, collision attacks on reduced SHA-256 stalled at 31 steps: Mendel, Nad and Schläffer's 2013 attack, at 265.5, set the record, and a practical collision for 31 steps followed only in 2024. Then 2026 brought rapid progress: the first 37-step attack in February (ePrint 2026/232), a practical collision attack on 35 steps in May (ePrint 2026/1080), and 38 steps at 2104.3 in June, in "Pushing Collision Attacks on SHA-2 to 39 Steps" (Li, Zhang, Li, Liu, Qian and Zhu, ePrint 2026/1120), which also gives the first colliding pair for 36 steps.

That June paper also gives the first collision attack on 39-step SHA-512, at 2178. For 39-step SHA-256 it finds a differential characteristic and a semi-free-start colliding pair, that is, a collision of the compression function from a chaining value of the attacker's choice rather than from the standard NIST initialization. It then counts 168 conditions on the state and message words from step 16 onward and concludes that its attack would cost 2168: "worse than the generic birthday attack", so "an ineffective collision attack on 39-step SHA-256".

Attack mechanism A differential collision attack takes two messages that differ in a few chosen places and follows a characteristic: a step-by-step prescription of how that difference spreads through the internal state and then cancels before the last step. Each step of the prescription imposes conditions on the actual values, and every condition the attacker cannot meet by construction has to be paid for by trying again. The attacks on SHA-2 in this line use two message blocks. The first is random and only supplies a chaining value; the second carries the difference. The densest part of the characteristic, where conditions are far too many to meet by chance, is solved once in advance. Each new chaining value is then connected to that fixed solution through the first few message words, and the conditions after the dense part are paid by trial.

The paper's framework fixes the dense solution for the early steps, including the first state words, so the only thing that changes between trials is the chaining value of the first block, and every condition from step 16 onward is paid by trial. At 38 steps that leaves 2104.3 of work. Shifting the same message-difference shape by one word to reach 39 steps moves a heavy word across the boundary into the uncontrolled part, and the trial cost jumps by about sixty bits.

Crowd-sourced research board

HashSmash is a public board where participants file reproducible attack packages against reduced-round hash functions. An automated AI judge checks each package mechanically, runs its declared sandbox experiments, reviews its argument, and scores it; a human reviewer then decides acceptance. Cost is measured in a fixed model: a classical word RAM with 256-bit words, in which one 39-step compression costs 2812 primitive operations and every other operation costs one. The score is the log2 of total charged work, counted in compressions and including preprocessing, the construction of any advice, failed trials and success amplification; memory is reported in bytes and penalised above one MiB. The model fixes conventions the literature leaves open, such as how many 32-bit lanes a 256-bit word may hold, and the board's numbers must be read with that in mind.

Until 17 October the 39-step frontier on the board was generic: birthday searches at 2124.83 (entry 744875ec, a 7-lane packed kernel, human reviewer accepted) and 2124.25 (Jbenisek's 0c1ef7c3, a 256-lane bit-sliced kernel, accepted by the AI judge). My own differential attempts sat above them. They used the published 39-step trail and solved its row-16 condition by a claw search rather than by trial, which was the right idea, but the tail probability I could measure for the full characteristic was about 2−79.8. My first filings of the claw, on 16 October, claimed to beat generic search; the AI judge refuted them, and on 17 October I withdrew that claim myself, because a pre-registered 140-seed study showed that the 20-seed tail estimate behind it had dropped a stalled run instead of counting it. With the corrected tail the attack cost 2124.25 of time plus a memory penalty, a score of 125.25: dominated by generic search, and filed as such. A second version, with a different bound on the same data, scored 124.39. Neither the retracted estimate nor these two scores enter the accepted result, which uses a different tail measurement (below).

The construction

The construction has four parts.

1. Leave the first two state words free. At 39 steps the local collision starts at message word W8, and the paper itself notes that W5, W6, W7 and E1, E2, E3 then carry no conditions. Neither do E4 and E5, the words the first two state words A0 and A1 feed into. So A0 and A1 need not be part of the fixed solution. Pin the state words of steps 2 to 15 of both members and the message words W10 to W15. For a chaining value cv from a random first block and any choice a0, a1 of the first two state words, the step function can then be inverted to read off W0 to W9, and both members follow the pinned trajectories through step 15 as an identity of the step function, not as an event with a probability. Each first block now yields 264 candidate second blocks instead of a handful. This is the freedom the paper's framework leaves unused, and the observation my first claw filing, on 16 October, was built on. At 38 steps it does not exist: the cluster starts at W7, E4 carries conditions, and only A0 is free.

2. Row 16 is a claw. The message expansion gives W16 = σ1(W14) + W9 + σ0(W1) + W0. With the dense part pinned, W9 is affine in a1, W0 is affine in a0, and a1 can be traded for u = W1. Exhaustive enumeration shows that the characteristic admits exactly two values of W16 and that only one of them, g, admits a row-17 continuation. The condition W16 = g then separates as F(a0) = F(u), where F is a single 32-bit map that takes one branch on even inputs and another on odd inputs. A pair of inputs, one from each branch, with the same output is a claw. Finding one claw by a birthday search costs about 216 evaluations of F; a distinguished-point walk in the style of van Oorschot and Wiener, which keeps running and harvests claws in bulk, gets them at about 211 evaluations each, amortised. That replaces 232 trials of a 32-bit condition. Every opposite-parity collision of the walk is a claw with W16 = g by identity, which the sandbox checks on every claw (no failure in 1,517,394). This is what the extra free word buys: with only a0 free, as at 38 steps, there is nothing to pair it with. The claw is the construction my filings of 16 and 17 October introduced.

3. Transplant the published pair, and make the rest exact. My filings carried their own dense solution (the published one with rows 0 to 3 re-solved, about 4 KB of characteristic data, and a solver able to produce such solutions inside one MiB). sraiw's filing (PR 421) took a cleaner route. It uses the semi-free-start pair of the paper's Table 23 itself as the only advice: 160 bytes, a chaining value and two message blocks that collide under the 39-step compression from that chaining value. It reads off the pair's dense part and transplants it onto a random first block from step 2. It then proves that the map from (cv, a0, a1) to (W8, W9, W16, ..., W23) is an exact bijection on 320 bits, triangular in five stages. Two consequences carry the rest of the attack. Over uniform (a0, a1), the words W8 and W9 are exactly uniform for every chaining value, so the side conditions below have exactly their counting probabilities. And the words W18 to W23 that drive the tail are a bijective image of free inputs, so the tail can be measured on uniform random draws and every hit inverted to an actual semi-free-start pair and checked. sraiw also proved the side-condition densities as active-bit theorems, showed that on the measured distribution "u odd" has probability exactly one half, so that the tail can be measured on exactly the inputs the claws produce (the "dual-parity fibre"), and measured the tail there.

4. Pay the rest. A claw gives a pair that follows the characteristic through row 16. Four more things must hold. Every filing in the line pays for the same four; the prices below are those of lamb356's accepted filing, each exact or measured:

About 2116.46 independent claws are therefore needed for success probability 0.39, the board's target. The walk delivers them at 3.65 compressions' worth of work each, about 21.87, which gives 2118.33; the filed 2118.35 adds statistical margins on the measured yield and cost.

The tail is the one quantity that is measured rather than counted, and the measurement does not run the attack. It samples the words W18 to W23 directly, with W8 and W9 drawn already inside F8 and F9 and the published E16 and E17 pinned, and lets the chaining value fall out of the bijection, read backwards. It estimates the probability row by row with sequential Monte Carlo over rows 18 to 23, which keeps resampling the survivors of each row, times the fraction of final proposals that end in equal states after step 38. Every hit is rebuilt into a pair and checked by two real compressions. The declared value is 2−75.67 (99% lower bound 2−75.59 over fourteen organizer-run replicas), and the hits are semi-free-start collisions from chaining values the sampler derived, which is why they are cheap: the attack has to take whatever chaining value a random first block gives, and there only one claw in 2117.48 completes.

Set against the paper's count, the difference is quantified. The paper pays 168 conditions on rows 16 to 38 by trial, at roughly one compression per trial. Here row 16 is not paid by trial at all; rows 18 to 38 are paid as the measured probability of the event, with several rows checked only on their signed differences, rather than as a count of bit conditions; and the work per trial is a few compressions spread over many claws. The price of the free words is the 9.81 bits of side conditions on W8 and W9, which the paper's fixed solution never pays.

Making it cheaper

The remaining work was engineering, and it is where most of the final score came from. sraiw's version walked F on 7 packed lanes of 36 bits and charged about 10.4 compressions per distinct claw, for 2120.46 in total. Jbenisek (PR 554) rewrote the walk as a bit-sliced program: 256 lanes, one bit of each lane per 256-bit register, so that rotations cost nothing and one step of F for all 256 lanes is about 955 straight-line records of five bytes. Every operation of the program runs through a counted machine, so the ledger is the program's own counter rather than a price table: 12,360 operations per distinct claw, 4.40 compressions, 2118.61 in total, in 1,047,548 bytes including the advice. lamb356 (PR 574) then shortened the kernel to about 723 records by restructuring the generator: sharing the rotation pair at distance 11 between Σ0 and σ0, specialising bit 0 by parity, and folding the constants, the branch selection and the final subtraction into one column-wise compression. That gave 3.65 compressions per claw and 2118.35 in total, in 1,047,892 bytes, with a Hoeffding bound on the total work over 2100.84 independent windows. Two later filings changed nothing in this program: mitchuski's (PR 845, 20 October) and geeknik's (PR 894, 21 October) spent more of the sandbox's time budget on the tail replica, which narrows the confidence bound on the same tail probability. They declare 2−75.60 and 2−75.47, for 2118.28 and 2118.15 in total; both are AI-valid and in review.

One evaluation of F costs a few word operations per lane when bit-sliced, and about 70 operations for seven lanes when packed; charging it as a 2812-operation compression is not a reasonable price. I report it because it is the one reading under which the comparison with the literature's 2128 needs no convention at all, and the attack still sits below. The 2126.8 figure counts only the first-stage walk; the second-stage re-walks that locate each claw add two thirds as many evaluations again, which gives 2127.6.

So under the board's model the construction beats the best accepted generic search by 6.5 bits, and the best generic search filed, which is still in review, by 5.9 bits. Under the harshest model it beats the birthday bound by about 0.4 bits. The first statement is the honest headline. The second shows the result is not an artifact of the accounting, but its margin is smaller than the tail's four-bit uncertainty: on my stricter tail the harsh reading would be about 2131.7, while the board-model reading would still sit 2.4 bits below generic search. The two readings should not be mixed: 2118.35 is a board-model figure and belongs next to 2124.83, while 2128 belongs next to 2127.6.

Assumptions

Every filing declares its score-critical premises. For lamb356's accepted filing they are: that the chaining value of a random first block behaves as an independent input to the walk and the later events, and that the yield and cost measured on 24 full windows extrapolate to 2100.84 of them; that a claw is a fair trial for the side conditions and for W17, where uniformity of W17 was tested on 310,224 real claws but only in its low 16 bits; that the tail probability measured on uniform draws of the fibre applies to claws; and that groups of claws with disjoint inputs are independent. Of the 117.48 bits of per-claw probability, 9.81 are exact counts, 32 rest on the uniformity of W17, and 75.67 rest on the tail. A correlation between claw selection and the tail would move the exponent one for one, and nothing testable at sandbox scale reaches it.

Why is the accepted tail so much larger than the 2−79.8 I measured? Partly because the events differ: my measurement exhausted the row-16 and row-17 words and divided out an exact 2−64 prefix, while the transplant pins the published values and measures only what follows; partly because of a relaxation at rows 19 and 20 worth 0.8 bits; and partly because the estimators and their treatment of stalled runs differ. The filings do not reconcile the two families of numbers, and the joint report does not either. The four-bit gap between them is the largest single uncertainty in the whole lineage, and anyone evaluating the result should know that. The two later filings rerun the same estimator with about three and five times the proposals per run; their means, 2−75.33 and 2−75.25, sit close to the earlier runs' 2−75.39, so they tighten the estimate within its family and do not touch the gap.

The published pair is free advice in the academic convention and must be constructed in the board's. The filings count its construction at 236 compressions: 234 candidates for the dense part, each tested for a non-empty row-16 and row-17 fibre in under four compressions. The paper's own figure for the corresponding step is 234.2. Whether 236 is the right price for "the characteristic plus one dense solution" is a fair question for review; it would have to exceed about 2118 to change the score.

Costs of developing the idea

Readers ask what an attack like this costs to produce. For my two early filings, the generic birthday entry 744875ec (124.83, 15 October) and the claw line through its first valid filing (125.25, 17 October), it took about 4 and about 40 hours of elapsed time. The first used under an hour of compute, a ledger and kernel-accounting exercise on an existing search. The second used about 140 core-hours on a 10-core local machine: 55.5 for the SAT campaign that re-derived the trail's shape, about 46 for the dense-base solver (34 of random restarts, 12.3 for the 1 MiB WebAssembly run), about 40 for the 140-seed tail study, and minutes for the 230-evaluation claw run. That is small by cryptanalytic standards, and it would have been smaller still without the trail re-derivation, which the board's cost model required me to account for. The figures come from the job logs in my run folders; the method is in the report's source map.

Limitations

No colliding pair for 39-step SHA-256 exists or will exist from this work; the ~2118 compression evaluations is not a computation anyone will run. The result does not touch full SHA-256, whose 64 steps keep a margin of 25 steps over this one. The number is a complexity claim under a stated cost model and declared heuristics, filed on a board and accepted by its reviewer, and it should be cited as exactly that until the technical report has been through review by people who did not write it.

Post-AI & quantum cryptography

Two families of post-quantum signatures are now standardized: lattice-based (ML-DSA, FIPS 204, with FN-DSA/Falcon still being standardized as FIPS 206) and hash-based (SLH-DSA, FIPS 205). Both are designed to resist quantum computers. They differ in what an attacker must defeat. Lattice schemes rest on structured algebraic problems; hash-based schemes rest only on the properties of a hash function.

Lattice-based schemes have a long record of failure, even though the core lattice problems have held. From GGH in 1999 and NTRUSign in its basic form in 2006 to EagleSign in 2023, scheme after scheme fell to attacks on its specific structure (Graph 4). In July 2026, an Anthropic researcher working with Claude Mythos Preview spent about 60 hours finding a hidden symmetry in HAWK's lattice; the resulting paper (Straznickas and Weis, ePrint 2026/1593) cuts HAWK-512's estimated key-recovery cost from 2150 to 2108. HAWK was the only lattice-based scheme in Round 3 of NIST's additional-signature process; its designers withdrew it the next day.

None of these results breaks the underlying problems (LWE and SIS) or ML-DSA, and HAWK's attackers state explicitly that ML-DSA and Falcon are unaffected. But the pattern is clear: lattice schemes fail through structure, and AI has now shown it can find exploitable structure that years of human review had not found. NIST anticipated the risk in 2022, when it said it was "primarily interested in additional general-purpose signature schemes that are not based on structured lattices," in order to diversify its standards.

Hash-based signatures have a different record. XMSS, LMS, SPHINCS+ and SLH-DSA have not been broken. The one notable cryptanalytic issue, a 2022 forgery attack on SPHINCS+'s category-five SHA-256 parameter sets, was fixed before FIPS 205, which uses SHA-512 in the affected functions at the higher security levels and keeps SHA-256 for the 128-bit parameter sets. Their foundation, SHA-256, has held for twenty-four years. Unlike MD5 and SHA-1, broken between 2004 and 2017, it uses a substantially stronger design, and no attack on full 64-step SHA-256 has done better than generic search. The attacks that would matter for hash-based signatures are of the preimage type, and on SHA-256 those reach fewer than 50 of 64 steps, at a cost close to the generic 2256. SLH-DSA's security against forgery does not rely on SHA-256's collision resistance, so the 39-step result has no bearing on it.

The 39-step result is itself AI-assisted work that found usable structure in reduced SHA-256. The difference is where that structure sits: 25 steps short of the full function, and in a property that SLH-DSA's security against forgery does not depend on.

My view. AI is now a working cryptanalyst, and so far it has been most effective at finding hidden structure. The ground is also moving faster than standards cycles: in 2026 the SHA-256 collision frontier went from 31 to 39 steps, and a Round 3 signature candidate was withdrawn the day after its attack was posted. That makes crypto-agility, the ability to swap a scheme or a parameter set without rebuilding the system around it, more important than ever. For long-lived assets I would pair that agility with the foundation that has the least structure to find: security that rests on the fewest and most-studied assumptions. That is a risk judgment, not a proof.

Next steps

Review. A joint technical report with sraiw, Jbenisek and lamb356 is in preparation: the construction stated once, in one notation, with every number mapped to the filing it comes from. It needs review by people who did not write it. The first thing review should press on is the tail, where the two families of measurements differ by four bits and the later filings tighten only one of them.

Other rows and earlier step counts. The claw works because two free state words meet in a single condition of the message expansion, at row 16. At 38 steps only one state word is free, so this placement does not exist there, but other pairs of free words, or a condition at another row, might give a claw in the attacks from 35 to 38 steps. The paper's 39-step SHA-512 attack uses the same message-difference shape; whether its first two state words are free as well, and whether a claw on its 64-bit row-16 condition would pay, is open.

Later step counts. Whether the construction reaches 40 steps is open; I expect it does not. It needs two inputs: a semi-free-start collision at the target step count, and a message-difference shape whose cluster starts late enough that the first two state words carry no conditions. Neither is known at 40 steps. No 40-step semi-free-start collision has been published, and the nine-word shape behind the 2026 attacks from 35 to 39 steps has, in the linearised message expansion, a solution space of dimension 2 at 37, 38 and 39 steps and dimension 0 at 40. The linearised model is not the whole space, though: the 37-step attack of ePrint 2026/232 uses a six-word signed pattern that it does not contain, so a usable 40-step shape could come from outside it. The closest known 40-step candidate inside it is the weight-26 codeword that Matusiewicz, Pieprzyk, Pramstaller, Rechberger and Rijmen found in their 2005 analysis of the message expansion as a linear code. It is the same shape with one extra bit on the chaining-value side: its expansion runs back before step 0, which, as they noted, means it cannot be used directly, and using it would need a first block that produces that difference, a near-collision nobody has costed. My working assumption is that the fixed-dense-solution accounting closed 39 steps, the free first two state words were the one currency it left unspent, and at 40 steps nothing obvious is left to spend. That is an assumption, not a result, and I am writing it up separately with the computations behind it.
